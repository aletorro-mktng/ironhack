# Content Playbook
**Alejandro Torres**

---

## Why this playbook exists
This playbook defines how content is planned, produced, distributed, measured, and improved under my leadership. It is designed to align **editorial quality, brand coherence, and performance** across channels and teams.


---

## 1) Objectives & North Star

Every piece of content must serve at least one of these objectives:

* **Brand authority** (credibility, trust, differentiation)
* **Organic growth** (SEO, discoverability, long-tail value)
* **Performance enablement** (assets that can scale in paid)
* **Community value** (engagement, saves, shares, retention)

If a piece does not clearly support an objective, it does not get produced.

**Operationally:**
Before green‑lighting any content, I require the objective to be written in one sentence at the top of the brief. If the team cannot articulate the objective clearly, the topic goes back to the backlog. This single step eliminates vanity content and keeps production aligned with business outcomes.

## 2) Audience & Intent

Tell Tales Ink plans content around **intent**, not formats.

Formats are chosen only after the intent is clear.

A Reel, carousel, blog post, newsletter, teaser, character spotlight, or ad variant should never exist just because the channel needs feeding. That is how content becomes noise with a Canva subscription.

For each topic, we define:

* **Who it is for**

  * Primary audience
  * Secondary audience

* **What they need**

  * A first hook into the story
  * A reason to care about a character
  * A clearer sense of the world
  * A theme they recognize in themselves
  * A reason to keep reading
  * A reason to buy, follow, subscribe, share, or return

* **What stage they are in**

  * **Discover:** They do not know the series yet. Give them atmosphere, tension, and one clear reason to look closer.
  * **Consider:** They know the premise or have seen the book before. Give them character stakes, thematic depth, folklore, reviews, comparisons, behind-the-scenes context, or proof that the story is worth their time.
  * **Decide:** They are close to buying, reading, joining the list, or following the series. Give them urgency, credibility, emotional payoff, and one clean next step.

**Tactical application:**

Intent is documented explicitly in the brief, in one paragraph, no more.

This prevents format-driven requests like:

“We need a Reel.”

And replaces them with intent-driven decisions like:

“This topic needs fast visual proof.”

“This character needs emotional context before the audience can care.”

“This theme requires depth and search visibility.”

“This moment needs a short, sharp hook because the audience does not know the world yet.”

“This piece needs credibility because the audience is close to buying but needs proof.”

**Example:**

A long-form Tell Tales Ink article about the Grim Cojuelo would not be written for “people interested in monsters.”

That is too vague and frankly, lazy with a little hat on.

The real intent might be:

“Readers who are curious about the cultural roots of the series and need to understand why the Grim Cojuelo is not just a killer costume, but a symbol of guilt, folklore, punishment, and Dominican identity.”

That intent justifies educational depth, cultural context, atmospheric language, and a commercial CTA without making the piece feel like it wandered in from a tourist brochure.

A similar approach applies to social content for Mortal Vengeance. Trend-led posts should only be produced when the trend can connect clearly to character, theme, folklore, media spectacle, or audience discovery. If a trend cannot bridge cultural relevance with story relevance, it should be deprioritized, even if it has short-term reach potential.

Reach without meaning is confetti in a crime scene.

It gets everywhere and explains nothing.

## 3) Editorial Framework: Default Flow

Unless there is a strong reason to deviate, Tell Tales Ink content follows this structure:

1. **Problem / Tension**
   A real, recognizable pressure point, emotional conflict, mystery, or audience question.

2. **Context / Education**
   Why it matters, where it comes from, and how it connects to the story, world, theme, or reader desire.

3. **Story / Solution**
   The book, character, world, theme, product, or idea as the logical answer.

4. **CTA**
   One clear next step.

This applies to blog posts, videos, emails, social posts, landing pages, character spotlights, and campaign assets.

For Tell Tales Ink, the “problem” is not always practical.

Sometimes the problem is:

* The audience does not understand the world yet.
* The character looks like a trope from a distance.
* The theme is powerful but needs a cleaner entry point.
* The folklore needs context before it can hit emotionally.
* The reader is curious but not ready to commit.
* The story has tension, but the marketing angle is too generic.
* The post sounds like any thriller, which is illegal in this house.

The job of the content is to turn that problem into movement.

Not just attention.

Movement.

From confusion to curiosity.

From curiosity to connection.

From connection to action.

## 4) Channel Strategy

Tell Tales Ink does not treat channels equally.

Each channel has a job.

Each channel has trade-offs.

Each channel should move the audience differently.

### Blog / SEO

* Evergreen over disposable news
* One clear search intent per piece
* Structured headings, internal linking, and scannability
* Written to be understood on first read
* Strong enough to serve as a long-term discovery asset
* Built around themes, folklore, character psychology, genre positioning, and reader questions

**My stance:**

Prioritize fewer, stronger evergreen pieces over volume.

A blog post is not a post.

It is an asset.

For Tell Tales Ink, blog content should deepen the world. It should help readers understand why the story matters, why the mythology feels specific, and why the themes have weight beyond the plot.

Good blog angles include:

* The Dominican folklore behind the Grim Cojuelo
* Why revenge stories need consequences
* How Catholic guilt shapes Mortal Vengeance
* Why silence is one of the series’ central villains
* How media spectacle changes trauma
* Why Caribbean Gothic deserves sharper genre positioning

A blog post should not feel like filler wearing an SEO trench coat.

### Social: Organic

* The first line decides everything
* One idea per post
* Built to be saved, shared, or remembered
* Native to the platform
* Clear emotional hook
* Specific story-world connection

**My stance:**

Fewer posts with a clear hook are better than daily noise.

Posting every day without intent is not strategy. It is panic with a content calendar.

Repurposing is allowed only if the piece feels native to the platform. A blog excerpt is not automatically a caption. A caption is not automatically a Reel script. A quote card is not automatically a campaign.

Each adaptation must earn its new shape.

For Mortal Vengeance, organic social should pull from:

* Character contradictions
* Moral questions
* Folklore details
* Scene-level imagery
* Reader reactions
* Awards and credibility
* Dark humor
* Behind-the-scenes author context
* Dominican cultural specificity
* The tension between revenge, justice, guilt, and public spectacle

Social should not flatten the series into:

“Dark secrets. Deadly lies. One shocking truth.”

That is thriller soup. We are not serving thriller soup.

### Video: Vertical-First

* Scripted for rhythm, not polish
* Subtitles are mandatory
* Clarity beats production value
* The hook must land fast
* The message should work without sound
* Each video should carry one idea

**My stance:**

Speed and clarity outperform cinematic quality.

The irony is delicious, yes, because the brand is cinematic. But vertical video is not a film festival submission. It is a knife fight in the attention economy, and the knife is the first three seconds.

For Tell Tales Ink, vertical video should be used when the idea benefits from immediacy:

* Fast premise hooks
* Character introductions
* “Read this if...” positioning
* Folklore explainers
* Theme breakdowns
* Before-and-after copy comparisons
* Book quote reveals
* Award announcements
* Trailer-style mood cuts
* Short author commentary
* Tell Tales Ink content strategy examples

Good vertical video logic:

“This needs fast visual proof.”

“This needs a face, voice, or rhythm.”

“This concept is easier to understand in motion.”

“This hook needs to hit before the viewer can scroll away and go watch someone organize a fridge.”

### Email

* One message per send
* Clear value exchange
* CTA matched to funnel stage
* Personal without becoming rambling
* Intimate, direct, and useful

**My stance:**

Email is not a dumping ground for content.

Every send must earn its place in the inbox.

For Tell Tales Ink, email should feel like the reader is being invited behind the curtain, not shoved into a sales funnel wearing a fake mustache.

Email can be used for:

* Book updates
* New releases
* Award announcements
* Behind-the-scenes notes
* Character spotlights
* Folklore deep dives
* Early access
* Reader community building
* Launch sequences
* Review requests
* Cross-promotion with related content

The reader should leave with something:

A sharper understanding.

A stronger attachment.

A reason to act.

A reason to stay.

## 5) Performance & Paid Alignment

Tell Tales Ink content is created to enable performance, even when it starts organic.

Organic content is not separate from performance.

It is the testing ground.

It shows which hooks, themes, characters, questions, and visuals create a pulse.

With paid or promotional efforts, align on:

* Hooks and angles to test
* Audience segments
* Naming conventions and UTMs
* Creative variants
* Platform-specific formats
* What content is worth scaling
* What proof points support conversion
* Which themes drive clicks, saves, shares, sales, or sign-ups

We learn from what performs and feed that insight back into editorial planning.

If a Mortal Vengeance post about the Grim Cojuelo gets strong saves, that may signal interest in folklore education.

If a character spotlight gets strong comments, that may signal emotional connection.

If a quote gets shares, that may signal positioning strength.

If a paid ad underperforms repeatedly, the answer is not always “change the button color.” Sometimes the hook is weak. Sometimes the audience is wrong. Sometimes the angle is too generic. Sometimes the creative has the emotional range of a wet napkin.

Performance should sharpen the strategy, not bully it into becoming bland.

## 6) Production Workflow: Process

This is how work moves from idea to publication:

1. **Topic and intent definition**
   What are we saying, who is it for, and why does it need to exist?

2. **Brief**
   Objective, audience, stage, format, key message, CTA, platform, source material, and success metric.

3. **Production**
   Writing, video, visuals, design, editing, or asset creation.

4. **QA**
   Brand voice, clarity, factual accuracy, rights, spoilers, platform fit, and CTA correctness.

5. **Distribution**
   Publish, schedule, promote, repurpose, or hand off to paid.

6. **Measurement and learnings**
   Review performance, identify patterns, document insights, and feed learnings back into planning.

Speed matters.

But quality gates are not optional.

A fast bad post is still a bad post. It just got there with cardio.

## 7) Measurement & Dashboard

Metrics only matter if they inform decisions.

Core metrics to track:

* Reach and growth
* Engagement
* Likes
* Comments
* Saves
* Shares
* Video retention
* CTR
* Website traffic contribution
* Email sign-ups
* Sales contribution where trackable
* Paid activation
* Content reuse
* Audience questions and comment themes

**How Tell Tales Ink uses them:**

Performance is reviewed on a fixed cadence:

* Weekly for tactical signals
* Monthly for strategic trends
* Campaign-end for deeper evaluation

A pivot is triggered when a pattern repeats across formats or channels, not because one post randomly escaped the basement and found sunlight.

Single-post outliers are not strategy.

Patterns are.

We separate signal from noise by looking for consistency:

* What people watch through
* What they save
* What they share
* What they click
* What they quote back
* What they ask about
* What gets reused in paid
* What helps move people closer to reading, buying, subscribing, or following

If a piece performs well organically, it becomes a candidate for paid testing.

If paid assets underperform repeatedly, the insight feeds back into editorial planning, not just creative tweaks.

Metrics inform decisions.

They do not replace judgment.

Vanity reporting is just a mirror with bad lighting.

## 8) Governance & Quality Control: Standards

Governance defines what good looks like.

Before publishing, verify:

* Brand coherence, both visual and verbal
* Alignment with the Mortal Vengeance voice
* Platform fit
* Spoiler safety
* Rights and usage permissions
* Clarity, structure, and factual accuracy
* CTA relevance and correctness
* Cultural specificity
* Tone accuracy
* Character and canon consistency
* Commercial purpose
* Accessibility where applicable, including subtitles and alt text

Process moves content forward.

Governance decides if it ships.

If it does not meet brand standards, it does not go out.

For Tell Tales Ink, quality control also means asking:

* Does this sound specific to this world?
* Does this have a clear audience intent?
* Does this create tension quickly?
* Does this make the story feel more distinct?
* Does this protect mystery without becoming vague?
* Does this respect the cultural material?
* Does this avoid generic thriller language?
* Does this move someone closer to action?

If the answer is no, the piece is not finished.

It may be drafted.

It may be pretty.

It may even have a cute little hook.

But it is not ready.

## Final Principle

Content is not output.

Content is **strategy made visible**.

For Tell Tales Ink, content should inform, position, seduce, unsettle, clarify, and move someone to act.

If it does not deepen the world, sharpen the message, build trust, create desire, or move the audience closer to the next step, it is not finished.

It is just noise in a nice outfit.
Tone Flexibility: Darkness Can Still Be Fun

Tell Tales Ink is dark, but it is not joyless.

The Mortal Vengeance universe has horror, guilt, grief, revenge, institutional rot, and psychological fallout. It also has sharp jokes, absurd timing, dramatic entrances, petty insults, family chaos, reality-TV energy, social-media spectacle, and characters who use humor because otherwise they would emotionally combust in public.

That tonal contrast matters.

The brand voice should allow fun when the content calls for it.

Fun does not mean goofy for no reason.

Fun means:

A sharper hook
A witty contrast
A darkly funny observation
A character-driven joke
A pop-culture reference that fits
A social post with bite
A caption that knows the situation is terrible and still finds the nerve to smirk

Tell Tales Ink can be:

Dramatic
Snarky
Stylish
Camp-aware
Media-savvy
Character-driven
Playful when the moment allows it
Brutal when the story demands it

The key is balance.

The humor should never erase the stakes.

The darkness should never erase the personality.

A Mortal Vengeance post can sound like a threat.

It can also sound like someone looked at the threat, adjusted their sunglasses, and said, “Well. That’s inconvenient.”

Fun, But Still On-Brand

Use a lighter or sharper tone when the content is about:

Character spotlights
Social posts
TikTok hooks
Behind-the-scenes author commentary
Reality-TV elements
Media spectacle
Reader reactions
Tropes and genre positioning
Book memes or playful comparisons
“Read this if...” content
Award announcements with personality
Posts designed for sharing, saving, or commenting

Keep the heavier tone when the content is about:

Trauma
Death
Abuse
Grief
Suicide references
Serious cultural or religious context
Institutional failure
Major emotional turning points
Spoiler-sensitive plot material

The rule is simple:

If the wound is fresh, do not make it cute.

If the scene is theatrical, social, petty, ironic, or media-driven, let it bite.

Examples of Fun Tell Tales Ink Voice

Too serious:

“Survivors must confront the lingering consequences of trauma and public exposure.”

Better:

“They survived the massacre. Then someone gave them a camera crew. Because apparently healing was too affordable.”

Too generic:

“This character is complicated and morally gray.”

Better:

“He has good intentions, terrible coping mechanisms, and the emotional regulation of a lit match near a gas stove.”

Too flat:

“The series explores media exploitation.”

Better:

“The trauma was private for about five minutes. Then the cameras arrived with lighting, contracts, and absolutely no shame.”

Too polished:

“Excelsior Academy is a prestigious school with a dark legacy.”

Better:

“Excelsior Academy had polished floors, perfect uniforms, and enough buried scandals to qualify as an archaeological site.”

Too salesy:

“Buy Mortal Vengeance today and experience a thrilling story.”

Better:

“Enroll at Excelsior Academy. The tuition is high, the trauma is generational, and the alumni network is mostly evidence.”

Fun Content Rule

Before using humor, ask:

Does the joke come from character, world, theme, or situation?
Does it make the post more specific?
Does it sharpen the hook?
Does it preserve the stakes?
Does it sound like Mortal Vengeance, not random internet noise?

If yes, use it.

If no, cut it.

Tell Tales Ink can smile.

It should just smile like it knows something you do not.
