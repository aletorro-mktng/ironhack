# Series Positioning

The *Mortal Vengeance* series is a psychologically driven thriller-horror saga set primarily in the Dominican Republic, where elite school culture, Catholic institutional pressure, personal revenge, Dominican folklore, social performance, and moral corruption collide.

The series should not be described as generic horror, generic YA thriller, simple slasher fiction, or straightforward revenge fantasy. Its identity comes from the collision between emotional damage and social systems: wounded teenagers, corrupt adults, suffocating institutions, public silence, class privilege, religious pressure, and the spectacle that follows when hidden sins finally surface.

At its core, *Mortal Vengeance* is about what happens when people who have been hurt begin to believe they are entitled to punish others. Across the series, revenge is never treated as clean justice. It may feel satisfying in the moment, but it corrodes everyone who touches it. The books examine the difference between justice, punishment, spectacle, and accountability, often through characters who are intelligent enough to know better and damaged enough to choose badly anyway.

The series blends psychological suspense, slasher tension, Caribbean Gothic atmosphere, Dominican folklore, dark academia, institutional horror, family tragedy, social satire, media spectacle, and morally complicated ensemble drama. Its horror is both external and internal: killers wear masks, but so do institutions, families, friend groups, and survivors trying to control how the world sees them.

# Core Series Identity

The *Mortal Vengeance* series is built around the idea that horror does not appear from nowhere. It grows inside systems that reward silence, protect reputation, excuse cruelty, and punish the people who speak too loudly.

The Grim Cojuelo is the central symbolic figure of the series. Inspired by Dominican folklore and reimagined through a darker psychological and slasher lens, the Grim Cojuelo represents guilt, punishment, spectacle, complicity, and the return of consequences. The mask changes meaning across the books. In Book 1, it becomes a figure of reckoning. In the prequel, it becomes a mythic expression of institutional rot and inherited guilt. In Book 2, it evolves into a symbol of violence in the age of cameras, edits, livestreams, gossip, and public consumption.

The series is Dominican in setting, atmosphere, humor, social texture, and cultural reference. Santo Domingo is not a decorative backdrop. It shapes the class dynamics, school culture, family expectations, religious pressure, public reputation, and social contradictions that define the story. The series should always be positioned as Caribbean Gothic and Dominican psychological horror-thriller, not as a generic masked-killer franchise with palm trees slapped on top like a tourist brochure with trauma issues.

## Long Description

*Mortal Vengeance* begins inside the suffocating pressure cooker of Excelsior Academy, where discipline, reputation, and silence shape student life. When Profesora Lourdes pushes her students past the point of endurance, a group of boys decide that humiliation deserves punishment. Alex, Marcos, Mario, and Manuel believe they are planning a lesson their teacher will never forget. They do not yet understand that revenge has a way of becoming larger than the people who start it.

Jealousy, resentment, social hierarchy, old wounds, romantic obsession, class pressure, and group loyalty twist the original act into something far more dangerous. The students’ friendships become weapons. Their secrets become liabilities. Their school becomes a stage where everyone is watching, pretending not to see, and quietly choosing sides.

Inspired by Dominican folklore and reimagined as a masked figure of punishment, the Grim Cojuelo turns private guilt into public terror. What follows is a slasher whodunnit about complicity, revenge, fear, emotional manipulation, and the terrifying question at the heart of the book: if you witness injustice and do nothing, are you innocent, or are you part of the crime?

*Mortal Vengeance* is the opening act of the series and the first major appearance of the Grim Cojuelo as a force of reckoning. *Mortal Vengeance: A Grim Tale* is the prequel to *Mortal Vengeance* and the origin story behind the moral, institutional, and folkloric nightmare that later consumes Excelsior Academy.

Set before the massacre, the novel follows Julián Díaz, a haunted senior whose life is shaped by guilt, Catholic pressure, academic cruelty, social isolation, and the suffocating silence of an institution determined to protect itself. Excelsior Academy presents itself as disciplined, elite, and morally superior, but beneath the polished surface lies a culture of humiliation, concealment, fear, and reputation management.

The horror of the prequel is not only what happens to Julián. It is the fact that what happens to him is made possible by a system. Students whisper because adults refuse to act. Abuse is hidden because scandal is treated as more dangerous than harm. Religious language becomes a tool of obedience. Academic excellence becomes a mask for cruelty. The adults who should protect children protect the institution instead.

Against this backdrop, the Grim Cojuelo evolves from Dominican folklore into something darker: a hunter of guilt, a symbol of everything the school refuses to confess, and a mythic shape for trauma that has nowhere else to go. Through Julián, Lucía, the younger students, the teachers, and the corrupt figures surrounding Excelsior, the book shows how silence becomes mythology, how mythology becomes violence, and how institutions create the evil they later condemn.

This is not simply the story of what happened before *Mortal Vengeance*. It is the story of why the horror became inevitable.

Mario remains the emotional conscience of the group, but Book 2 places him inside a system that does not reward sincerity. Reality TV wants conflict, confession, humiliation, and meltdown. Mario wants people to be okay, which is adorable and strategically disastrous. His arc explores kindness under pressure, family trauma, guilt, and the difficulty of staying gentle when the people around you are rewarded for becoming crueler versions of themselves.

## Lucía Salgado

Lucía’s return is one of the major narrative engines of Book 2. For years, she existed as a missing figure, a mystery, a source of evidence, and an unresolved wound. Now she returns as a living person with trauma, agency, secrets, and the right to decide what parts of her story belong to the public. Shane wants Lucía because she represents television gold. Mónika protects Lucía because she understands that truth without consent is just another extraction. Lucía’s arc explores revelation, control, survival, and the right to speak on one’s own terms.

## Lieutenant Ricardo García

Ricardo enters Book 2 carrying grief that no case closure could resolve. He is no longer only the investigating officer connected to the original tragedy. He is a man shaped by loss, memory, duty, and the failure to protect everyone he loved. His role grounds the sequel because he represents the difference between real grief and televised grief. In a story full of cameras, Ricardo carries the kind of pain that resists editing.

## Shane Harper

Shane Harper is the sequel’s central media antagonist. She is brilliant, manipulative, ambitious, and morally allergic to boring television. Shane does not merely document conflict. She manufactures the conditions under which conflict becomes inevitable. She sees trauma as material, people as arcs, pain as footage, and instability as opportunity. Her function is crucial because she embodies the sequel’s core question: when suffering becomes profitable, who is responsible for what happens next?

## Ana Sofía

Ana Sofía expands Melissa’s emotional world beyond the original Excelsior group. She represents chosen family, intimacy, and the possibility of a life not entirely defined by the massacre. Her presence matters because the show does not only exploit the core survivors. It reaches into the people they love, the lives they built afterward, and the relationships that were supposed to remain outside the frame.

## Valeria Viccini

Valeria is one of the new cast members who understands that reality television is a game of image, leverage, and controlled cruelty. She is verbal, socially agile, ambitious, and more vulnerable than her performance initially suggests. Valeria complicates the “villain” role because she knows she is being hired to provoke, but she also understands that being hated publicly should come with compensation. Her arc explores survival strategy, class pressure, and the cost of performing cruelty for opportunity.

## Camila Álvarez

Camila is sharper, colder, and more openly transactional than Valeria. She does not pretend the show is innocent. She knows what Shane wants from her and negotiates accordingly. Camila represents the professionalization of reality-TV villainy: cruelty as labor, backlash as occupational hazard, and public hatred as a billable service. Her clarity makes her dangerous because she does not need to lie to herself in order to participate.

## Rafa Santos

Rafa is hungry for attention, validation, and relevance. He enters the show eager to prove he belongs, but Shane quickly teaches him that sincerity is less valuable than spectacle. His arc explores performative masculinity, insecurity, romantic posturing, and the way reality television rewards the loudest, ugliest, most exaggerated version of a person. Rafa is not evil by default, but the show knows exactly which buttons to press.

## Elías Sarraff

Elías carries unresolved romantic, social, and masculine resentment into Book 2. He represents wounded entitlement: the kind of man who experiences rejection as humiliation and humiliation as justification. His presence complicates the old dynamics between Alex, Melissa, and the broader survivor group. Elías’s function is to expose how pride, image, and resentment can masquerade as love or justice.

## Doña Silvia de Herrera

Doña Silvia is Alex’s mother, manager, protector, and occasional one-woman crisis management department with lipstick. She provides humor, warmth, authority, and emotional grounding, but she is not simply comic relief. Her role matters because she sees the danger of the show before many others do. She knows Alex’s capacity for self-destruction, and she refuses to let Shane turn her son into content without maternal resistance.

## Humberto Salazar

Humberto Salazar carries the legacy of Profesora Lourdes and Sister María Gracia into the sequel. As Lourdes’s son and María Gracia’s nephew, he exists at the intersection of grief, institutional consequence, and unresolved anger. His role complicates the moral landscape because he grieves someone who was both victim and perpetrator within the broader history of Excelsior. Humberto represents inherited damage and the dangerous ways grief can search for a target.

## Father Ángel / Padre Ángel

Father Ángel remains one of the series’ clearest embodiments of predatory authority and institutional failure. In Book 2, his shadow remains over the story because his escape from justice proves that exposure is not the same as accountability. His presence reminds readers that while the public obsesses over reality-show drama, the deeper corruption that created the original tragedy has not been fully resolved.

## Sister María Gracia

Sister María Gracia represents silence, retreat, religious complicity, and institutional self-preservation. Her withdrawal after the collapse of Excelsior does not erase her importance. It intensifies it. In a sequel obsessed with confession and exposure, María Gracia’s refusal to speak becomes part of the moral pressure surrounding the story.

## Principal Davis Beltrán

Principal Davis Beltrán remains central to the aftermath of Excelsior Academy even from prison. His punishment does not equal full accountability, and his presence allows Book 2 to examine the difference between one man taking the fall and an entire institution being forced to confront what it enabled. Beltrán represents administrative cowardice, reputation management, and the arrogance of adults who confuse distance from violence with innocence.

## Profesor Máximo

Profesor Máximo expands the sequel’s critique of institutions beyond Excelsior Academy and into higher education. As head of the law department, he holds power over Alex’s future and becomes part of the system through which production, academia, and reputation collide. His role shows that leaving Excelsior does not mean escaping institutional pressure. It just means the corruption wears a different blazer.

## Don Rolando Stinga

Don Rolando is Mario’s father and one of the sequel’s most volatile supporting figures. He brings family trauma, machismo, humiliation, addiction to attention, and generational damage into the reality-show environment. His presence forces Mario to confront the painful difference between loving a parent and being safe around them. Don Rolando also shows how family dysfunction becomes even more destructive when cameras turn it into spectacle.

## Don Ramón

Don Ramón is tied to grief, vengeance, haunted spaces, and the older Gothic architecture of the series. He represents the wounds that remain beneath the cameras and celebrity chaos. His role connects Book 2 back to the deeper mythology of Excelsior, the cathedral, and the unresolved pain that predates the reality show. Don Ramón gives the sequel a necessary sense of old horror under new spectacle.

## Doña Yvelisse de Rocha

Doña Yvelisse is Melissa’s mother and one of the key adult presences grounding the sequel in family consequence. She is protective, sharp, emotionally expressive, and not easily impressed by romantic drama masquerading as maturity. Her role is especially important because she pushes back against the way Melissa’s life is being consumed by public narrative, relationship chaos, and the expectations placed on young women.

## Luis Rocha

Luis Rocha expands Melissa’s family context and helps prevent her from being defined only by romance, survival, or public image. His role matters because family members in Book 2 are not safely outside the story. They are pulled into the spectacle, exposed by association, and endangered by the same machinery consuming the survivors.

## The Grim Cojuelo

In Book 2, the Grim Cojuelo returns in a world transformed by cameras, confessionals, livestreams, social media, and public obsession. The mask no longer represents only guilt and punishment. It now also represents the way violence becomes footage, trauma becomes content, and vengeance becomes spectacle. Public-facing materials should tease the Grim Cojuelo’s return without confirming identity, motive, mechanics, number of killers, or the final meaning of the mask.

# Cross-Series Thematic Progression

*Mortal Vengeance* is about complicity before and during the bloodshed.

*Mortal Vengeance: A Grim Tale* is about the institutional rot that made the bloodshed possible.

*Mortal Vengeance II: To Reel or Not Too Real?* is about what happens when the bloodshed becomes content.

This progression should guide all series-level positioning. The series is not repeating the same horror engine three times. It is expanding the site of power: first the classroom, then the institution, then the camera.
